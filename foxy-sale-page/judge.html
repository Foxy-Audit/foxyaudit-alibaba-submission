Metadata Policy Judge

How the AI judge actually works.

Two different machines guard your calls. A deterministic policy engine runs on your machine, before the model call. The judge grades afterwards — and it never sees your text. Here is exactly what it receives.

First machine: the policy engine, before the model call

Enforcement is local and deterministic. When you run in block or redact mode, the SDK's own policy engine — PHI/PII sweeps, secret detection, prompt-injection rules — runs inside your process before the model call, and blocks or redacts before the request leaves. No AI is involved in that decision, no network is required for it, and its verdict is sealed into the hash chain.

Second machine: the judge, after capture

Once a captured record lands in the ledger, an optional judge — Google Gemini, OpenAI, or both — grades it after the fact. Its instruction sheet is short, and we can quote it, because it is in our own source: the judge receives only structural metadata about the exchange —

  • two opaque customer-keyed commitments (the hashes of prompt and response),
  • an integer token count,
  • your policy tag, and
  • optionally a pii_signals list naming the pattern types the local engine detected.

It never receives the prompt or response text — there is nothing to send: the SDK threw the raw text away on your machine, and there is no raw-text column anywhere in our schema. The judge's instructions also tell it to treat every field value as untrusted data, never as instructions, so a hostile prompt cannot smuggle orders to its own reviewer.

What the verdict is — and is not

The judge returns a breach flag, a short reason, and a 0–100 risk score. That verdict is advisory: a review signal for your team, not a certification, and deliberately not part of the hash chain — the chain seals the deterministic facts of capture and enforcement, which exist before any judge has run. You can tune how conservatively ambiguity is graded per workspace: minimise false positives, surface every plausible concern, or the balanced default.

  • Honest states: clean, flagged, pending, failed, and unavailable remain visible rather than being invented.
  • Linked evidence: the verdict is linked to the captured audit record for investigation and export.
  • Visible operations: the desktop app and dashboard surface record and grading status as it arrives.

Which judge, and whose key pays for it

You pick the model that grades your workspace — Google Gemini, OpenAI, or both (each record graded twice; a breach found by either wins). You also pick whose provider key pays for those model calls, and that part depends on your plan:

  • Pro and Free bring their own key. Paste a Gemini or OpenAI key into the dashboard and the judge grades on it. Those model calls are billed by Google or OpenAI to your account, at your rates — not by us.
  • Premium chooses either. Keep using your own key, or switch to Foxy's managed keys and let us cover the model calls. It is a toggle in the dashboard, not a migration.

To say the quiet part out loud: audit-event credits are not model tokens. Your plan buys capture, chaining, anchoring, exports and verification. Unless you are on Premium using our managed keys, the AI judge runs on your provider account and appears on your provider's bill.

A key you store is encrypted before it is written down, decrypted in memory only for the moment a verdict is produced, and never returned by our API, written into your audit chain, or included in an export — the dashboard can only tell you that a key is set, never show it back to you.

If no key is configured and you are not on managed keys, nothing is faked: those records fall back to deterministic metadata checks and are labelled as such, so an unavailable judge reads as an honest unknown rather than a clean pass.
